What the headline is made of

Contributions are expenditure-weighted category inflation, with weights renormalised monthly over the valid basket — the same convention the momentum model uses. They sum to the sum of contributions, not to the published headline: a 12-month rate compounds while summed monthly contributions are linear. The readout reports both and the gap.

By group

Biggest surprises vs AR benchmark

What am I looking at?

Each month, every category contributes weight × its own inflation to the headline. Stacking those by major group shows what the print is actually made of, and how the mix changes.

A rolling AR(p) benchmark then says what each category's inflation should have been, given its own history. The residual is the part nobody could have read off the past — the surprise. Because inflation splits exactly into forecast plus residual, each contribution splits exactly into expected and surprise, and the two always add back to the bar.

"Surprise" here means deviation from the category's own rolling AR(p) forecast — the working paper's definition of a shock. It is not a deviation from a published consensus or a nowcast; nothing on this site carries those.

Coverage. Four gauges carry their official taxonomy in the category code itself, so the grouping is a property of the data and not a mapping anyone maintains by hand: US CPI (the BLS major group is the third character of the stratum code, SEF* → food & beverages) and UK, France and Germany (COICOP divisions, from 04.1 and CP0116 respectively). US PCE, Japan and Canada use flat codes — DNEAR, 0021, P40 — that encode no parent. Their hierarchies do exist, but in the pipelines: Japan's is walked from the e-Stat cat01 parent tree, Canada's is the parent_id column of its category config, and PCE's is the BEA line/level structure. None of it reaches ism.json, which ships only key and label. Adding a group field to the exporter switches those three on with no change to this page. Until then they are shown ungrouped rather than bucketed by guesswork.

What am I looking at?

For each of ~130 disaggregated PCE categories we fit a rolling AR(p) benchmark for monthly inflation and keep the latest residual. A category has positive momentum when its last k residuals are all positive, negative when all negative. The ISM index is the expenditure-weighted positive share (S⁺) minus the negative share (S⁻).

What am I looking at?

For each PCE category we estimate a rolling reduced-form VAR of its log price and log quantity on J lags of both (Eqs. 12-13), and keep the one-step residuals. A category-month is demand-driven when the price and quantity surprises move the same way (both up or both down), and supply-driven when they move opposite ways. The supply/demand contributions to inflation are the expenditure-weighted (Laspeyres) sums of those categories' inflation (Eq. 15); the year-over-year version is the running 12-month product. Raising the precision cut moves near-zero residuals into an ambiguous bucket — the variant FRBSF publishes.

The decomposition needs category quantity as well as price. For the US, only the BEA PCE underlying detail provides this monthly (headline and core). Other countries publish it in their quarterly national accounts, so the same estimator (4 lags, 40-quarter window) runs on those via the Scope selector: Canada (Bank of Canada variant, Kang–Sekkel–Taskin–Yang SAP 2026-33; total / goods / services), UK (ONS Consumer Trends), France (INSEE), Germany (Eurostat, by durability) and Japan (Cabinet Office SNA, by type). Everything is recomputed client-side by a parity-tested port of the Python engine; drag J, the window, the precision cut or the category set and watch it update.

Trimmed mean & median

This month's cross-section

Weights that moved most

What am I looking at?

Each month the categories are sorted by their seasonally adjusted, annualised price change and lined up along their expenditure weight. Throw away the lowest α and the highest β of that weight and average what is left: that is a trimmed mean. Push both cuts to 50% and you are left with the single category holding the middle of the basket — the weighted median, which is why the Cleveland Fed can name it each month.

Trimming is not cosmetic. Cross-sectional price changes are fat-tailed, so the plain mean — headline inflation — is a noisy estimate of its own centre. The trim buys a large variance reduction for a small bias, and the bias is removed by choosing the cuts to track a smooth trend, which is how the Dallas Fed arrived at cutting 24% off the bottom and 31% off the top: the PCE cross-section is right-skewed, so symmetric trimming inherits a downward bias.

Everything is recomputed client-side by a parity-tested port of the Python engine. Our Cleveland-cut median reproduces the published median CPI at a 12-month correlation of 0.997 and the 16% trimmed mean at 0.998; the PCE trim reproduces the Dallas Fed series at 0.990 since 1998 (0.997 on the full sample).

CPI to PCE bridge

What adds up to it

Why this can be done at all

The CPI and the producer price index for a month are published about two weeks before the PCE for the same month. Between those two dates most of the PCE is already determined and simply not yet assembled: shelter comes straight from the CPI, food and energy nearly so, and the pieces that do not follow the CPI — medical services, portfolio management, air fares — follow producer prices that are also already out.

So the estimate is not a forecast of prices. It is a translation of readings that already exist into the aggregate BEA has not published yet, group by group, with each group's mapping fitted only on months that came before the one being estimated.

What am I looking at?

PCE inflation has run about 0.3pp below CPI inflation since 2000, and the Fed targets the lower one. The Why they differ view splits that wedge into the four things that generate it — a different aggregation formula, different weights over the same spending, different price sources for the same goods, and different scope — plus the CPI lines PCE has no counterpart for, and an explicitly reported rebuild residual. Each term is one substitution in a chain from the published CPI to the published PCE, so the six add up to the gap by construction.

The weight term is the one that moves: shelter is roughly a third of the CPI basket and a sixth of PCE, so the 2021-22 rent surge added well over a point to CPI relative to PCE and then went into reverse. The price term is the one that persists, and it is mostly medical care — which is also why the producer price index does most of the work in the nowcast.

The first four are the paper's subject. Belgium, Italy and Spain are reference cases: λ barely moves inside any one country, so the indexation interaction is identified across the panel, and Belgium and Germany share a currency, a central bank and the 2022 energy shock while differing in exactly the institution under study.
Spiral gain is the headline object. Effective wage is what a household actually received. Indexation is the institutional database. Simulator closes the loop. Handouts is the 2021-26 scheme register.
Income composition weights from CBO (US), ONS (UK), INSEE (France) and Destatis EVS (Germany). The bottom quartile takes 39% of its income from indexed benefits in the United States and 52% in Germany; the top quartile takes none from the statutory floor.
The long-run pass-through is one by construction — the homogeneity restriction makes the Phillips curve vertical — so the gains are always read at a finite horizon. Three years is the horizon the euro-area pass-through literature reports.
λ is not measured. It is documented coverage weighted by an assumed pass-through per channel: 0.90 for a contractual escalator (Card 1986), 1.00 for statutory public pay, 0.35 for a minimum-wage-benchmarked agreement (Cengiz et al. 2019 put spillovers at about 40% of the wage gain), 0.30 for a formal inflation reference (ECB OP 299). These four numbers carry the institutional story, so they are the first thing you should be able to disagree with.
The fiscal dial is percentage points of household income handed out per point of inflation above trend. The monetary dial is slack opened per point of excess inflation — a reduced-form sacrifice rate, not a Taylor rule.

What this model is

The other four models on this site break a price index apart. This one asks the question from the other side: when prices move, what happens to what households are paid — and does that feed back?

Three things pay a household, and only one of them is a wage. There is the market bargain; there is the statutory floor, which in France has been indexed to prices by formula since 1970 and in the United States has not moved since 2009; and there are indexed benefits and pensions, which cover about 70 million Americans and rose 8.7% in a single January in 2023. Since 2021 there is a fourth: discretionary payments and energy subsidies worth several percent of GDP across Europe, some of which enter the consumer price index and some of which do not. The effective wage index adds all four, weighted by an income group's actual composition.

The spiral gain is the product of two pass-throughs: how much of a real-wage loss gets recovered (Λ), and how much of a wage impulse reaches prices (M). A spiral needs both. Belgium has the highest indexation intensity in the panel and among the lowest wage-to-price pass-through, which is why automatic indexation there did not produce excess inflation; Italy under the scala mobile had both, and a gain of 0.76.
